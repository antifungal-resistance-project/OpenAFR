# The Antifungal Resistance Project — Business Plan

## Overview

The Antifungal Resistance Project is a nonprofit foundation working toward a world
without antifungal resistance. Our first program develops new medicines for
drug-resistant fungal infections.

Our plan is straightforward. We have built software that identifies promising drug
candidates for one of the most important antifungal targets. As those candidates
are validated in the laboratory, we develop the best of them into a medicine
through a small, dedicated company that the foundation controls. The foundation's
ownership is structured so that the mission stays in charge for the life of the
program: the medicine is developed and priced to reach the patients who need it,
and the returns are reinvested in the mission rather than paid out to owners.

This document explains how the organization is built, how the science becomes a
medicine, how we fund the work, and where the project stands today.

## The problem

Fungal infections kill an estimated 2.5 million people a year, and the drugs we
rely on are failing. The most widely used antifungals, the azoles, work by
blocking a single fungal enzyme. A growing number of pathogens have evolved around
that block. *Candida auris* is the clearest warning: it spreads in hospitals,
resists multiple drugs at once, and the World Health Organization has placed it in
its highest-priority category of fungal threats.

New antifungals are genuinely hard to make. A drug has to kill a fungal cell
without harming the human cells around it, and fungi are close enough to us
biologically that this is a narrow line to walk. As a result very few companies
work in the field, and the pipeline of new medicines is thin at exactly the moment
resistance is rising.

## Our approach

Most computational drug discovery ranks candidate molecules by a predicted binding
score. Those scores are notoriously unreliable. Our software takes a different
approach: it ranks candidates by whether they can physically reach and engage the
part of the target enzyme that the drug needs to shut down. That is the actual
mechanism by which these drugs work, so it is a more honest measure of whether a
molecule has a real chance.

The tool is built to prove itself before it is trusted. Before it ranks anything
new, it has to rediscover the drugs we already know work, on data it has never
seen. If it cannot, it does not run. This discipline is deliberately conservative,
and it is what separates a credible shortlist from a list of guesses.

The result is a triage tool. It narrows a large library of molecules down to a
short list worth testing in a laboratory. A high rank is a hypothesis, not a cure.
The laboratory decides.

## From a candidate to a medicine

Turning a promising molecule into an approved drug is a long road, and we are
honest about that. The path runs through a series of clear checkpoints, and we only
commit money to the next stage once the previous stage has genuinely earned it.

1. **Laboratory validation.** The first real test. Top candidates are made and
   tested against live, drug-resistant fungal strains, and checked to confirm they
   harm the fungus without harming human cells. Most candidates do not survive this
   step, and that is expected.
2. **Lead development.** Survivors are refined into a small family of related
   molecules with better potency, safety, and drug-like properties.
3. **Preclinical development.** The best molecule is put through the safety and
   manufacturing work required before it can be tested in people.
4. **Clinical trials.** Testing in humans for safety and effectiveness.
5. **Approval and delivery.** Bringing the finished medicine to patients, typically
   in partnership with an established pharmaceutical company for the final trials
   and manufacturing.

Each stage costs more and takes longer than the last. Our job is to reach each
checkpoint as efficiently as possible and to stop, and say so plainly, if a program
does not clear one.

## How the organization is built

The project is built in two parts that are deliberately kept separate.

**The foundation** is a nonprofit. It has no shareholders and no owners, which
means it cannot be bought or sold. It holds the mission, the core intellectual
property, and control of the development company. If the foundation ever closes,
its assets pass to another organization with the same purpose, never to
individuals.

**The development company** is where the drug development work happens. It is a
public benefit corporation, a type of company whose directors are legally required
to weigh the public benefit, in this case getting antifungals to the people who
need them, alongside financial return. The company can sign licensing deals, take
milestone payments, and, when necessary, accept investment, all of which a pure
nonprofit struggles to do.

The connection between the two is what keeps the mission secure. The foundation
holds a special class of shares in the company that carries control of key
decisions, even if outside investors later provide most of the funding and hold
most of the financial upside. Investors can earn a fair return. What they cannot do
is take control of the mission, force a sale, or override the commitment to
patient access. This foundation-controlled structure is well established; it is the
same arrangement that lets one of the world's largest pharmaceutical companies
operate under the long-term control of its founding foundation.

## Keeping the team small

We intend to keep the development company small. A handful of people hold the
science, the strategy, and the intellectual property. The laboratory work,
chemistry, manufacturing, and clinical trials are carried out by specialist
contract research organizations under agreements that assign all resulting rights
back to the company.

This is not only a way to control cost. A small organization keeps its running
costs low, and low costs are themselves a protection: an organization that does not
have to feed a large payroll is far less likely to be forced into a decision that
compromises its mission.

## How we fund the work

We prioritize funding that does not require giving up ownership or control. In
order of preference:

- **Grants and public health funding.** Government research grants and dedicated
  programs for fighting drug resistance. Several of these are designed for small
  organizations and pair them with academic laboratories, which also helps supply
  the laboratory capacity the early work needs.
- **Foundations and prizes.** Philanthropic funders and prize programs focused on
  drug resistance and neglected diseases. This is mission-aligned capital that
  supports the work without seeking to own or redirect it.
- **Mission-aligned investment.** When the later, more expensive stages require
  investment, we accept it only in a form that preserves the foundation's control
  and the commitment to patient access.
- **Partnership and licensing.** The final and most expensive stages of clinical
  development are normally carried out with an established pharmaceutical partner.
  In that arrangement the partner funds the late trials and manufacturing, and the
  foundation receives payments and royalties in return.

Money that comes back to the foundation is reinvested in the mission: the next
medicine, and in time the wider work of diagnosis and surveillance. The goal is for
the mission to compound, not for any individual to profit.

## Where we are today

The software exists and has been validated on published data. What it has not yet
had is its first laboratory test. The single most important thing the project needs
now is a laboratory partner, a clinical or academic group able to test our leading
candidates against live drug-resistant strains. That one relationship unlocks
everything that follows.

Alongside it, we need a modest amount of grant funding to pay for that first round
of testing, and the go-ahead to formally establish the foundation and adopt its
charter now, while the organization is small and its structure is simple to put in
place.

## What comes next

Over the coming year our priorities are, in order:

1. Establish the foundation and adopt the charter.
2. Secure a laboratory partner and the funding for the first round of testing.
3. Test our leading candidates and report the results honestly, whatever they show.

If the results are encouraging, we protect the discovery and move into lead
development. If they are not, we return to the software with a new set of candidates
or a new target. Either way, the tool and the organization remain, and the mission
continues.
